Michael Mofina rabbit · product design · 2024 – 2025

Crafting AI-powered interfaces for rabbit.

From 2024 to early 2025 I was the main product designer on rabbitOS, the interface of the r1 — including the rabbitOS 2 redesign that runs on every r1 today. I worked with a couple of design colleagues and the industrial, brand, and engineering teams, and I designed the web experiences for teaching and directing the r1's agent: teach mode and the LAM playground. The screens below aren't screenshots. I rebuilt each one in code for this page.

rabbitOS — from wheel to touch

The r1 launched with a constraint set from the top: the entire interface had to be navigated with the scroll wheel and a single button. No touch — even though the hardware had a perfectly good touch screen.

It sounded like a fun brief, and at first it was. But every comparable device we studied, the ones that genuinely worked without a touch screen, spread the load across several physical controls. We had one button, so everything in the system collapsed onto a single click, a double click, or a long press. Typing is where the constraint fell apart: you can't type with a wheel, so we made an exception and let people type on the touch screen, which gave the game away — it had been there all along. The timeline was short and the constraint wasn't mine to lift, so that's what shipped. The launch UX wasn't good. Reviewers said so, and they were right.

Then the problem got worse. The product kept gaining features (ordering food, playing music, more every month) and each one landed on an input model that was already past its limit. Patching it one screen at a time was never going to catch up.

rabbitOS 2 was the reset: a card interface built for touch, with the wheel kept as a secondary input, because the wheel is the most charming thing about the hardware. Each card does one thing, with a glyph drawn in the brand style — we built out a full icon set. There's nothing to memorize; you scroll until you see the thing you want. I designed it up to my last day at rabbit in early 2025, my colleagues took it the final mile, and it shipped shortly after. It's what runs on every r1 today. Pick one up and you're using this design.

teach mode — teaching the agent by showing it

We started teach mode in 2024, before "agent" was a buzzword. Chat was still how everyone talked to AI, and there was no pattern to reference for what teaching an agent should look like, so we worked it out from first principles. The idea: open a virtual machine in your browser, describe what you're about to do, do it once, and rabbit turns the recording into a lesson it can repeat. Lessons could be shared with the community. The part I still think was right is that you could trigger a lesson from the r1 itself — ask the device in your pocket to order a pizza, and the lesson runs on a machine in the cloud while you get on with your day.

I designed the whole teach mode experience in rabbithole, the r1's companion portal: the landing and lesson library, recording sessions, community lessons, and the replay flow. It worked, but not reliably. So most of the design effort went into the moments around an agent that fails a lot: checking what it actually learned, handing off logins before a replay, and making "lesson failed, retry" a normal part of the loop instead of a dead end.

LAM playground — describe it instead

While teach mode was in alpha, agent models were improving fast enough to skip the demonstration entirely. That became the LAM playground, which we ran alongside teach mode as a second experiment: type what you want, and the agent does it on a virtual machine while an action log narrates each step in plain language next to the live view. You can pause mid-task to sign in, redirect it with a new instruction, and resume. It launched in rabbithole, and like teach mode, you could kick off a task from the r1 and let it run in the background.

Running the two side by side taught me something I still use in agent work: for most tasks, describing what you want works better than demonstrating it. But neither approach matters if the person can't see what the agent is doing and stop it when it goes wrong.

Since rabbit

I'm at Doist now, designing Todoist. The last thing I shipped is ramble: you talk out loud and your task list builds itself as you speak; change your mind mid-sentence and it edits what it already wrote. I'm also the sole designer on a new automations product, not yet launched, where you describe what you want in natural language and agents assemble a repeatable automation connected to your tools over MCP. Of everything on this page, it's the closest to the rabbit experiments above: the same problems, with much better technology.

Day to day I work as much in code as in Figma. I ship my own PRs with Claude Code, and my prototypes tend to be working software rather than clickthroughs. This page is one of them.
